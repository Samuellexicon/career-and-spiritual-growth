Spiritual progression portfolio

Guiding principles

Enduring power through covenant keeping and deep faith
"He is like a man which built a house, and digged deep, and laid the foundation on a rock." — Luke 6:48

Lasting strength, whether spiritual or professional, comes only through deep and genuine rootedness rather than surface-level preparation. Elder Johnson's image of the empty vessel that catches fire because it has nothing inside to absorb what is directed at it captures the danger of shallow technical knowledge and shallow faith alike. The same channels that carry God's power into a disciple's life are opened through covenant keeping, diligent study, and genuine conversion. This principle shapes how I approach every certification, every system design, and every professional challenge: depth is not optional.

Co-creation with God through revelation and intentional planning
"For I, the Lord God, created all things… spiritually, before they were naturally upon the face of the earth." — Moses 3:5

Effective work begins with spiritual creation before physical execution. This principle reshapes how I approach every infrastructure deployment, every business proposal, and every career decision. Beginning with the end in mind is not simply a productivity strategy; when grounded in faith it becomes an act of submission to God's superior knowledge of where I am going and what I am capable of becoming. Sister Johnson's invitation to let Christ author our stories reinforces this: yielding our professional narrative to the Master Author is the highest exercise of agency, not an abandonment of it.

Disciple-leadership as a moral and relational calling
"That which is of God is light; and he that receiveth light, and continueth in God, receiveth more light." — D&C 50:24

Leadership in every domain is always a moral act with real consequences for real people. Elder Clark's framework of generating light and driving out darkness defines the standard I want to hold myself to in every IT role I occupy. As a systems administrator I will hold the security, reliability, and privacy of systems that people and organizations depend on. That is not a neutral technical responsibility. The three questions Elder Clark posed — what is my purpose, how can I lift those around me, how can I add value — are the questions I carry into every professional setting, every team interaction, and every ethical decision I face in this field.

Reflective journal

Faith and technology: observations on the interplay

During a recent period of quiet reflection, I found myself thinking about legacy in a way I had not anticipated. Watching Elder Stevenson speak about the Okinawa Japan Temple and the way temple ordinances connect families across generations, one question kept returning to me: what am I building, and will it outlast me? In my electrical maintenance work at Volta Aluminium, I maintain systems that keep a major production facility running. If I do my job well, those systems continue to function reliably long after my shift ends. In IT, the same principle operates at a larger scale. The infrastructure I design, the security policies I implement, and the documentation I write will shape the environments that future administrators inherit. The temple, as a place where eternal covenants are made and families are sealed across time, is the ultimate expression of building something that outlasts the present moment. That realization deepened my sense of professional purpose in a way I had not expected.

A second insight came from reflecting on the danger of taking blessings for granted. The same complacency that can creep into temple attendance can creep into the daily habits that sustain growth in any domain. As I approach the end of my degree, I am aware that the structured learning environment this course has provided will soon give way to the less structured rhythms of professional life. The discipline of spiritual and professional growth that IT 497 has cultivated must become self-sustaining after graduation. The integration of my spiritual and professional identities is not a project that ends with this course. It is the ongoing work of a disciple-leader who understands that systems, whether technical or spiritual, require active monitoring, regular maintenance, and a commitment to continuous improvement. That is the professional I am committed to becoming.
